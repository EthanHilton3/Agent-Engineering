# Homework 2d: Embeddings

## Summary

For this one I played around with OpenAI's `text-embedding-3-small` to get a feel for what an embedding model thinks "similar" means. I started small, comparing pairs of words and sentences, and worked up to a bigger project: finding every place the Book of Mormon quotes the Bible, using nothing but embeddings.

My main takeaways: embeddings are great at telling what a text is *about*, and not great at the details of what it *says*. ("I love my dog" and "I hate my dog" come out pretty similar.) The size of the text you embed changes everything. And in the quote project, almost every "mistake" the embeddings made turned out to be a mistake in the answer key, which I did not see coming.

## 1. Setup

An embedding turns text into a list of 1,536 numbers. The way it finally clicked for me was to think of it as an arrow: texts with similar meanings point in similar directions. The arrows all have length 1, so comparing two texts is just a dot product, which gives you a score from about 0 (unrelated) to 1 (the same).

All the scripture text came from a public JSON copy of the Standard Works. Here's how the pieces fit together:

| File | What it does |
|---|---|
| `emb.py` | The shared helper: embeds text, caches the results, loads the scriptures |
| `exp1_pairs.py` | Scores groups of pairs: paraphrases, negation, other languages, typos, abbreviations, code, gibberish |
| `exp2_search.py` | A little search engine over the BoM, D&C, and PoGP, plus queries meant to break it |
| `exp3_chunking.py` | The same questions at four chunk sizes: phrase, verse, 5 verses, chapter |
| `exp4_crossrefs.py` | Auto-generated cross-references for a few verses |
| `intuition.py` | Five small experiments where I guessed first and wrote down what happened after |
| `exp5_hidden_copies.py` | The Bible-quote finder, in four parts (A–D), with my guesses and results in the comments |

One thing that made all of this way easier: **we cached every embedding.** The first time a piece of text gets embedded, `emb.py` saves the vector to its own little file on disk, named after a hash of the text. After that, it just loads the file instead of calling the API again. Embedding all 31,000 Bible verses took about 3 minutes the first time, and every run after that was basically instant and free. That meant I could rerun an experiment as many times as I wanted, change the output, or tweak a threshold without paying to embed the same verses over and over. By the end, the cache had over 50,000 vectors in it, and the whole assignment cost well under a dollar.

Claude wrote the code, including both answer keys for exp5, and I reviewed it and ran things. Claude ran exp1–exp4 first to show me the basics. For `intuition.py` and exp5, I wrote down a guess before every run, and honestly that's where I learned the most.

## 2. Similar and dissimilar inputs

Some pairs that stuck with me:

| Pair | Score |
|---|---|
| "I love my dog" vs "I love my puppy" | 0.91 |
| "I like cheese" vs "Me gusta el queso" | 0.77 |
| "I love my dog" vs "I hate my dog" | 0.73 |
| "USA" vs "United States" | 0.69 |
| "baptism" vs "thermodynamics" | 0.20 |
| "The car needs new tires" vs "Quarterly earnings beat expectations" | 0.10 |

A few things I noticed:

- **Topic matters more than logic.** Love vs hate made more of a difference than I expected, but those sentences still came out fairly related because they're about the same dog. Negation was even weaker: "I do not love my dog" scored 0.76 against "I love my dog," higher than "hate" did.
- **Meaning matters more than language.** "Adios" was closer to "goodbye" (0.72) than to "hola" (0.51).
- **Small wording changes add up.** "I really love my dog" scored *lower* than "I love my puppy." Swapping in a synonym barely costs anything, but adding a word shifts the whole arrow.
- **Typos and abbreviations mostly work.** "definately" vs "definitely" was 0.83. But "WoW" for Word of Wisdom matched verses with "wo unto" in them, lol.
- **Code works across programming languages.** The same `add` function in Python and JavaScript scored 0.77.

The one that surprised me most was that **nothing is ever really 0**. Two random arrows came out at −0.001, because random numbers just cancel each other out. Real text doesn't. "Pizza recipe" vs "the Treaty of Versailles" was 0.02, but keyboard gibberish vs a line of Python was 0.18. Is code that related to gibberish?? My best guess is that the arrow picks up the *form* of the text too, and code and gibberish are both weird non-sentences. So what counts as "unrelated" depends on what you're comparing.

I also tried the famous king − man + woman = queen trick. King still won, and queen came second. I should have seen that coming: man and woman are almost the same arrow, so swapping one for the other barely moves you away from king. The demos you see online leave the input words out of the answers, which is how queen ends up winning.

## 3. The effect of text size

This was the biggest lesson for me. **One chunk of text gets one arrow,** so the arrow ends up being a blend of everything in the chunk.

To see it happen, I took "Nephi built a ship to cross the ocean" and buried it in more and more filler about budgets and printers, then searched for that same sentence:

| How much filler | Score |
|---|---|
| none | 0.97 |
| 1 block (about 24 words) | 0.63 |
| 8 blocks | 0.38 |
| 32 blocks (the sentence is 1% of the text) | 0.20 |
| filler only | 0.02 |

It drops off a cliff after the first block and then slowly slides. I guessed it would hit "unrelated" after 4 or 5 blocks, and it never got there. But 0.20 is way below anything you'd actually search with, so in practice the sentence is lost.

The same thing showed up in the scriptures:

- Verses found specific things best, and chapters found big themes best.
- Mosiah 13 quotes the Ten Commandments in 13 of its 35 verses. Comparing whole chapters, Exodus 20 came back at rank **110**. Comparing verse by verse, those verses were found easily.
- Matthew 6:13 is one verse, but 3 Nephi splits it into two. Each half got compared with the whole Bible verse, so both scored low even though the words are identical.

## 4. Where search fails

Normal questions worked well. "Forgiving people who wronged you" found the forgiveness verses, and "build a boat" found 1 Nephi 17. The failures were more interesting:

- **It always returns something.** "Airplane" matched a verse about seraphim flying. There's no "nothing found" unless you set a threshold.
- **The Spanish query flopped.** "Hable con dios" (I talked with God) only got weak, mostly off-target matches, even though Spanish and English pairs matched fine on their own.
- **It ignores "NOT."** "Wicked people who were NOT destroyed" just returned general verses about wickedness and judgment. It didn't pick up on the "NOT" at all.

## 5. The big project: Bible quotes in the Book of Mormon

The Book of Mormon quotes long chunks of the Bible: Isaiah 2–14 in 2 Nephi, the Sermon on the Mount in 3 Nephi, Malachi, and more. That gave me something the other experiments didn't have: an answer key. So I embedded all 31,000 Bible verses and checked whether the embeddings could find the copies by themselves.

**Chapter by chapter**, every near-copy found its Bible twin at rank 1, mostly scoring above 0.97. I guessed about 0.95, so even closer than I expected. The only miss was Mosiah 13, the dilution problem from above.

**Verse by verse**, I turned it into a quote detector: for each of the 6,604 Book of Mormon verses, find the closest Bible verse, and if the score is over a threshold, call it a quote. This is where I learned about **precision** (when it says "quote," how often is it right?) and **recall** (of all the real quotes, how many did it catch?). I thought the strict 0.95 threshold would have the best recall, but it's the opposite: a higher bar lets fewer verses through. It has the best *precision*. You can't max out both, and I ended up picking 0.85 as the best balance.

### Turns out the answer key was the problem

The first answer key was a list of whole chapters that Claude wrote from memory. When I looked at where the embeddings and the key disagreed, the key was wrong every single time:

- **It missed real quotes.** The top 15 "false alarms" were all actual quotes the key left out, like 2 Nephi 30 quoting Isaiah 11, and 3 Nephi 21 quoting Micah, which I didn't even know was in there.
- **It counted new text as quotes.** It marked all of 3 Nephi 12 as copied, but some of those verses aren't in the Bible at all.

So Claude built a second key, and this time **without embeddings**, since grading the embeddings with a key the embeddings made would be cheating. It used plain text matching instead, which was harder than it sounds. Exact phrase matching broke on tiny differences like "Woe" vs "Wo," and it got fooled by short verses like "And he said unto him, Yea." It took a smarter word-by-word comparison, a few judgment calls, and checking all 71 new quotes by hand before it was trustworthy.

Then I graded the exact same results against both keys:

| Threshold | Precision, old key | Precision, new key | Recall, new key |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.95 | 91% | 100% | 79% |
| 0.90 | 90% | 100% | 90% |
| 0.85 | 88% | 99% | 94% |

Nothing about the detector changed, only the grading, and precision jumped about 10 points. A bad answer key made a good system look worse than it was.

Even with the new key, a few disagreements were left, and they still mostly weren't the embeddings' fault. The 6 extra verses it flagged were paraphrased quotes, which text matching can't see but embeddings can. And of the 35 quotes it "missed," 31 had actually matched the right verse; they just scored under 0.85 because of added words like "Verily, verily, I say unto you."

My favorite example was 3 Nephi 20:35. It's word for word Isaiah 52:10, except "The LORD" becomes "The Father," and it adds "and the Father and I are one." It scored only 0.72. One clause changes the meaning that much, and the embedding cares way more about meaning than about matching words.

## 6. What I learned

1. **Embeddings capture what a text is about.** Topic and meaning come through strongly. Negation and small details don't.
2. **Scores are relative.** There's no universal "unrelated" number, so rankings are more trustworthy than raw scores, and every threshold has to be tuned.
3. **Chunk size decides what you can find.** One chunk, one blended arrow. Small chunks find specific things, big chunks find themes, and a short passage inside a big chunk basically disappears.
4. **The search was almost always right. The threshold was the weak spot.** The right verse was the top match about 99% of the time. Nearly every miss happened when a cutoff turned that ranking into a yes or no.
5. **Answer keys have blind spots too.** The key from memory missed quotes, and the text-matching key missed paraphrases. I only figured out who was right by comparing all three methods and looking at where they disagreed.
6. **Precision and recall trade off.** A strict threshold is right when it fires but misses things. A loose one catches everything, plus a lot of junk.
7. **Cache your embeddings.** Saving every vector meant I never paid twice for the same text, so experimenting was fast and basically free.
